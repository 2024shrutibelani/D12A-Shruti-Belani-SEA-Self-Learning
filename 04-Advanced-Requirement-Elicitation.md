
# Advanced Requirement Elicitation Techniques

## 1. Introduction

Requirement Elicitation is the process of collecting information about the needs, expectations and requirements of users and stakeholders.

It is an important activity in Software Engineering because it helps developers understand what the customer actually wants from the software system.

Advanced Requirement Elicitation Techniques help software engineers collect accurate and detailed requirements using different methods.

Two important techniques are Interviews and Ethnography.

## 2. What are Requirement Elicitation Techniques?

Requirement Elicitation Techniques are methods used by business analysts and software engineers to understand and collect requirements from stakeholders.

These techniques help identify user problems, business needs and expected system features.

### Objectives

- Understand customer requirements.
- Identify user expectations.
- Reduce misunderstandings.
- Discover hidden requirements.
- Improve communication with stakeholders.
- Develop software according to user needs.

## 3. Interview Technique

An Interview is a requirement elicitation technique in which a software engineer or business analyst directly communicates with stakeholders to collect information.

The interviewer asks questions to understand the needs, problems and expectations of users.

### Types of Interviews

**1. Structured Interview**

In this type, the interviewer prepares a fixed set of questions before the interview.

Example: Asking all bank employees the same questions about an existing banking system.

**2. Unstructured Interview**

In this type, the interviewer asks open-ended questions and allows the discussion to continue naturally.

Example: Discussing with a customer about their experience using a mobile application.

**3. Semi-Structured Interview**

It combines structured and unstructured interviews. Some questions are prepared in advance, but additional questions can also be asked.

### Steps Involved in Interviews

1. Identify the stakeholders.
2. Prepare interview questions.
3. Schedule the interview.
4. Conduct the interview.
5. Record important answers.
6. Analyze the collected information.
7. Document the requirements.

### Advantages of Interviews

- Direct communication with users.
- Helps understand user expectations.
- Allows clarification of doubts.
- Provides detailed information.
- Helps identify hidden requirements.

### Limitations of Interviews

- Time-consuming process.
- Answers may be incomplete or unclear.
- Requires proper preparation.
- Different stakeholders may have different opinions.

## 4. Ethnography Technique

Ethnography is a requirement elicitation technique in which the analyst observes users while they perform their daily activities in their actual working environment.

Instead of only asking questions, the analyst studies how users actually work.

This technique helps discover requirements that users may forget to mention during interviews.

### Example

Suppose a company wants to develop a Hospital Management System.

The analyst visits a hospital and observes doctors, nurses and receptionists while they perform their daily activities.

The analyst studies:

- How patients register.
- How doctors access patient records.
- How nurses update patient information.
- How appointments are managed.
- What problems employees face during their work.

Based on these observations, the analyst identifies the actual requirements of the hospital system.

### Steps Involved in Ethnography

1. Select the working environment.
2. Identify the users to observe.
3. Observe their daily activities.
4. Record their working methods.
5. Identify problems and difficulties.
6. Analyze the observations.
7. Convert findings into software requirements.

### Advantages of Ethnography

- Helps understand real user behaviour.
- Identifies hidden requirements.
- Provides practical information.
- Helps discover problems in existing processes.
- Improves understanding of the working environment.

### Limitations of Ethnography

- Requires considerable time.
- Observation may affect user behaviour.
- Requires skilled analysts.
- Some activities may be difficult to observe.

## 5. Comparison Between Interviews and Ethnography

| Comparison Parameter | Interviews | Ethnography |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Meaning | Direct communication with stakeholders through questions. | Observing users in their actual working environment. |
| Main Focus | Understanding user opinions and expectations. | Understanding actual user behaviour and activities. |
| Method | Asking questions and discussing requirements. | Watching and recording daily activities. |
| Interaction | Direct conversation with stakeholders. | Mainly observation, sometimes with interaction. |
| Information Collected | User opinions, needs and suggestions. | Actual working methods and practical problems. |
| Time Required | Depends on the number and length of interviews. | Usually requires more time. |
| Best Used When | Requirements need to be discussed directly. | Actual user activities need to be understood. |
| Example | Interviewing students about a college management system. | Observing students using the existing college management system. |

## 6. Real-Life Application

### Project: College Management System

Suppose a college wants to develop a new College Management System.

**Using Interviews:**

The analyst interviews students, teachers and administrative staff.

Questions may include:

1. How do students check attendance?
2. How are examination results published?
3. What difficulties do teachers face while managing records?
4. What features should be included in the system?

The answers help identify the required features.

**Using Ethnography:**

The analyst observes the daily activities of college staff and students.

The analyst studies:

1. How attendance is recorded.
2. How students submit assignments.
3. How examination records are maintained.
4. How administrative staff manage student information.

These observations help identify practical problems and hidden requirements.

## 7. Difference Between Traditional and Advanced Requirement Elicitation

| Traditional Techniques | Advanced Techniques |
|:---|:---|
| Mainly collect basic requirements. | Help discover detailed and hidden requirements. |
| Often depend on direct questioning. | May include observation and deeper interaction. |
| May not identify actual user behaviour. | Help understand actual user behaviour. |
| Examples include basic questionnaires. | Examples include interviews and ethnography. |

## 8. Conclusion

Advanced Requirement Elicitation Techniques are important for understanding the actual needs of users.

Interviews help analysts collect information directly from stakeholders, whereas Ethnography helps them understand users by observing their daily activities.

Both techniques help identify correct requirements, reduce misunderstandings and improve the quality of software development.
